# What is a spiking neural network?
Previously, we saw how spiking neurons approximate the behavior of biological neurons by integrating incoming signals and generating spike trains. In this chapter, we explore how individual spiking neurons can be connected to form spiking neural networks, or SNNs. We first introduce the fundamental concepts of network connectivity, then demonstrate how these principles can be used to construct fully connected and convolutional SNN architectures. Throughout the chapter, we focus on the structure and forward dynamics of these networks, leaving the question of how their parameters are learned to subsequent chapters.

## 1.5.1 Connecting Spiking Neurons
Just as biological neurons communicate with one another through synaptic connections, artificial spiking neurons can be connected together to form networks capable of more complex computations. This section introduces the fundamental building blocks of an SNN, focusing on how neurons communicate and how their connections can be mathematically represented. We then examine how different patterns of connectivity determine the organization and behavior of the resulting network.

The fundamental building block of an SNN is the synaptic connection, which allows spikes generated by one neuron to influence the activity of another.

### 1.5.1.3 Connectivity patterns

## 1.5.2 Building a fully-connected SNN
Now that we have established how spiking neurons can be connected, we can use these principles to construct our first complete SNN. ## 1.5.3 Building a convolutional SNN
While fully connected SNNs provide a straightforward way to connect populations of neurons, they can become inefficient when processing large, spatially structured inputs such as images or event-based visual data. Convolutional SNNs address this limitation by exploiting the spatial organization of their inputs, using locally connected neurons and shared synaptic weights to extract features. In this section, we extend the principles developed for fully connected networks to construct convolutional spiking layers and combine them into a complete convolutional SNN.

### 1.5.3.1 Spatially structured inputs and connectivity
Many types of sensory information have an underlying spatial structure, motivating network architectures that preserve and exploit relationships between neighboring inputs.

- Discuss the limitations of fully connected networks for spatially structured inputs, particularly the number of connections required.
- Introduce local connectivity as a method for restricting each neuron to receive input from a particular spatial region.

### 1.5.3.2 Convolutional filters and receptive fields
Convolutional networks build upon local connectivity by applying the same set of synaptic weights across multiple spatial locations, allowing similar features to be detected throughout the input.

Spiking neural networks combine the dynamics of individual spiking neurons with structured synaptic connectivity to perform computations over space and time. In this chapter, we explored how neurons can be connected and how these connections can be organized into fully connected and convolutional architectures. Although these architectures differ in their connectivity patterns, they rely on the same underlying principles of synaptic communication, neuronal state updates, and spike generation. The examples presented here demonstrate how SNNs process information using predefined network parameters. In the following chapters, we will explore how these parameters can be adapted through local and global learning to produce desired network behaviors.
